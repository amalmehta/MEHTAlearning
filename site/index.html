MEHTA: PEARL latents meet long-horizon MAML

An agent should spot which world it is in from a few steps of experience and keep improving with gradient steps when that world is new to it. PEARL does the first; MAML does the second. MEHTA does both, and is meta-trained for long post-training.

Amal Mehta · October 2026 · Small-scale pilot: 2-D tasks, 3 seeds, CPU only

Short version: latent inference plus post-training clearly beats either alone. The specific new piece, a meta-learned initialization for post-training, showed no measurable gain over plain fine-tuning, either in v1 or after a diagnosed redesign (v2, fresh seeds). v2's training fix did make long sparse episodes solvable: from 0% to 27–60% success. v3 then tested a shift chosen (using the baseline only) to be hard for plain fine-tuning: MEHTA v2 started from a better point but did not adapt faster, and did not beat compute-matched PEARL + fine-tune.

PEARL

A latent variable z, inferred from recent transitions, tells the actor which environment it is in. Adaptation is zero-gradient and fast, but it can only pick among worlds it saw during training.

MAML

One meta-gradient from many environments learns an initialization that improves quickly under ordinary gradient steps. It keeps improving on new worlds, but needs data and steps for every task.

MEHTA

Infer z first, then post-train from a MAML-learned initialization for many steps. The meta-gradient is taken through a long inner loop, and a proximal pull to the initialization is meant to limit forgetting across a long sequence of tasks.

What the pilot found

Point-Goal and Point-Waypoints, seeds 0–2, post-training up to K = 400 steps. "Better" means the mean gap exceeds the summed across-seed standard deviations.

  • The latent is what matters most. All latent methods beat first-order MAML without a latent by a wide margin (out-of-distribution goals: MEHTA −13.9 ± 1.4 vs MAML −29.6 ± 1.6). Without task inference, MAML's initialization walks toward the average training goal, which is the wrong way for goals below the axis.
  • Post-training helps beyond zero-shot inference. MEHTA beats PEARL alone on all three task sets, most clearly under shifted dynamics (−6.9 ± 0.1 vs −11.7 ± 1.6).
  • The long-horizon MAML step did not measurably help. MEHTA vs PEARL + naive fine-tune, which uses the same post-training from a PEARL initialization: no clear difference on any task set. A long vs short inner loop (50 vs 5 steps): also no clear difference. MEHTA's results were more consistent across seeds (e.g. shifted dynamics ±0.06 vs ±0.66), but with 3 seeds that is a hint, not a finding.
  • Continual: less forgetting, but not because of the proximal term. MEHTA forgot less than PEARL + naive fine-tune (−0.40 ± 0.67 vs 1.12 ± 0.26). Removing the proximal term did not increase forgetting (−1.11 ± 0.12), so the gain does not come from it.
  • Long sparse episodes: a negative result for every method. No method completed the 3-waypoint task on any test goal, and sparse-reward post-training did not fix that.

Method

MEHTA stands for Meta-gradient + Embedded Hidden-Task Adaptation. It keeps PEARL's machinery whole [2]: a permutation-invariant encoder maps each transition (s, a, r, s′) to a Gaussian factor; their product is the posterior q(z | c); a soft actor-critic [5] conditions both actor and critics on z. To that it adds a first-order MAML [1] meta-update of the actor/critic initialization, in Reptile form [3], taken through a long inner loop with an iMAML-style proximal term [4].

Meta-training (each iteration)
  collect episodes on 5 training tasks: z ~ prior, then z ~ q(z | c)
  repeat 75×  # PEARL step
     sample 4 tasks; z ~ q(z | c) with reparameterization
     SAC critic loss + 0.001·KL(q ‖ N(0, I)) → critics, encoder
     SAC actor loss with z detached           → actor
  every 2nd iteration  # long-horizon MAML step (new)
     for 2 tasks i:  θᵢ ← θ;  z = mean q(z | cᵢ)
        repeat K_inner = 50×:  θᵢ ← θᵢ − α∇[ SAC(θᵢ; z, test-time reward) + λ/2‖θᵢ − θ‖² ]
     θ ← θ + β · meanᵢ(θᵢ − θ)

Post-training on a new task (test time)
  explore 2 episodes → infer z                      # PEARL: K = 0
  for k = 1..K: every 10 steps collect an episode; re-infer z from all context
               one SAC step on this task's data + λ/2‖θ − θ_meta‖²

What is new, and what is not

Combining a task latent with gradient adaptation is not new in itself. CAVIA [6] adapts context parameters by gradient descent, and MAESN [7] meta-learns a latent exploration space alongside a MAML policy. MEHTA's specific combination is: (1) an amortized, probabilistic, off-policy PEARL posterior for zero-shot task inference, (2) a meta-gradient through a long first-order inner loop, motivated by the short-horizon bias of truncated meta-optimization [8], and (3) proximal post-training that serves as both the iMAML regularizer and a guard against forgetting in a continual task sequence. Whether this combination pays off is an empirical question, and the pilot below is the first test.

Implementation choices, not taken from the papers: SAC v2 (twin Q-functions, learned temperature) instead of PEARL's original value-network SAC; the MAML baseline is first-order and off-policy (a SAC inner loop), not the original policy-gradient MAML; KL weight 0.001 instead of PEARL's 0.1 (PEARL used reward scale 100, which shrinks the KL's relative weight about 10⁴×; at scale 1 the 0.1 setting held the encoder near the prior); initial SAC temperature 0.1; hidden width 64; 300 iterations × 75 updates. These were picked or checked on a separate development seed and not swept.

Limits of this pilot

  • Tiny tasks. A 2-D point mass with 20–60 step episodes. Nothing here says how MEHTA behaves on high-dimensional control or real robots.
  • Three seeds, five tasks per set, one scored episode per task. The score at K = 0 is noisy: the same PEARL model scored −6.9 and −11.7 in two evaluations, depending on its exploration episodes.
  • Not compute-matched. Meta-training gradient steps: PEARL 22,500; MEHTA and MAML 37,500 (including inner-loop steps); MEHTA with a short inner loop 24,000.
  • Under-trained. Validation return was still improving at iteration 300. CPU time was shared with other heavy workloads, so each run took about 75 minutes.
  • Chosen, not tuned. λ = 0.5, β = 0.3, K_inner = 50. A development seed was used only to fix the training failures described in the method note.
  • Point-Waypoints needs a better exploration story. Dense shaping during meta-training, as in PEARL's sparse protocol, was not enough at this budget.

Version 2: why the MAML step did nothing, and a fix

We re-ran v1 MEHTA's meta-training (Point-Goal, seed 0) with instrumentation on all 150 MAML steps. The numbers show why it couldn't help:

  1. Nothing to learn. Once the latent identified the training tasks, gradient steps barely improved them (+0.39). On new goals, test-time post-training gains roughly +4 to +7. MAML was practicing adaptation on tasks that didn't need it.
  2. Too small to matter. The MAML step moved the weights about 7% as much as PEARL's own updates, so the initialization stayed essentially PEARL's.
  3. The signal averaged away. Updates from different tasks were nearly orthogonal, so averaging them mostly cancelled out.

Raw data: results/diagnostics/v1_inner_loop_s0.json (uv run python -m mehta.diagnose_v1).

What changed

  • MEHTA v2's inner loop runs the test-time protocol (fresh episodes, online collection, test-time reward, proximal pull) on training tasks with random dynamics perturbations: action gain 0.5–1.5 and a constant drift up to 0.03 per step. The latent was never trained on these, so the gradient steps have a residual to fix. Test shifts (new goals, 60° rotation) are a different family and are never seen in training.
  • The MAML step runs every iteration with step size 0.5 (was every 2nd, 0.3).
  • Compute-matched baseline: PEARL gets 100 updates per iteration, equal to MEHTA v2's 51,200 sample-gradients per iteration (checked by a test).
  • Control, PEARL-DR: PEARL trained with the same perturbations in half its episodes. If it matches MEHTA v2, the gain comes from the perturbed data, not from MAML.
  • Waypoints shaping is now potential-based (Ng, Harada & Russell, ICML 1999), which provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged. v1's shaping made hovering just short of a waypoint about as good as reaching it.

These changes were designed after seeing v1 fail, checked on a development seed (100), then run once on fresh seeds 3–5 that played no part in any decision. v1's results above are unchanged.

Results on fresh seeds 3–5

  • The MAML step still doesn't help. MEHTA v2 vs compute-matched PEARL + fine-tune: no clear difference on in-distribution goals, new goals or shifted dynamics. Against the control, PEARL-DR + fine-tune, MEHTA v2 is worse under shifted dynamics (−6.84 ± 0.34 vs −6.32 ± 0.16). The perturbed training data is what helps; the meta-update adds nothing on top.
  • Most of the headroom is on new goals. An oracle reference (a greedy controller that knows the goal and the dynamics; an estimate, not a proven optimum) scores about −3.8. Post-trained methods close 55–73% of the remaining gap on in-distribution goals, but only about 30% on new goals (≈ −15). Corrected 2026-10-21: an earlier version of this page put the ceiling at about −4.5 and called in-distribution goals "at the ceiling"; the measured oracle shows they are about 0.6–1.1 short of it.
  • Long sparse episodes now work, for every method. With potential-based shaping, waypoint success went from 0% for every v1 method to 27–60% (PEARL-DR 60% ± 28%, PEARL + fine-tune 53% ± 25%, MEHTA v2 27% ± 9%; no clear differences at 3 seeds). This is a fix to the training signal, not a MEHTA result.
  • Continual: no clear difference. The proximal term again increased forgetting (0.33 ± 0.58 vs −1.08 ± 0.58 without it).

Where this leaves the idea: in these tasks, gradient post-training from PEARL's own initialization works as well as any meta-learned initialization we tried. Showing MEHTA's value would need tasks where post-training from PEARL's initialization is clearly too slow, and a fresh set of seeds. Seeds 3–5 are now spent and won't be reused for tuning.

Version 3: a shift where plain fine-tuning is too slow

If fine-tuning from PEARL's initialization is already nearly as good as possible, no initialization can beat it. So v3 looks for a held-out shift where that fine-tuning is clearly too slow. The shift is chosen using only the PEARL baseline, by a rule fixed before any run (pre-registered in the spec):

  1. On development seed 100, train compute-matched PEARL and fine-tune it for 400 steps on each candidate shift. None of the candidates is ever used in training.
  2. Gap closed = (return at K=400 − return at K=0) ÷ (oracle − return at K=0). The oracle is a greedy controller that knows the goal and the dynamics, an estimate rather than a proven optimum.
  3. Pick the candidate with the lowest gap closed, among those still at least 30% short of the oracle. MEHTA is not evaluated during selection.

Then PEARL (compute-matched), PEARL-DR and MEHTA v2 are each trained once on fresh seeds 6–8. Primary hypothesis: MEHTA v2 beats both fine-tuned baselines on the chosen shift at K=400, by the same rule as before.

Results on fresh seeds 6–8

  • The pre-registered hypothesis is not supported. On mirrored actions at K=400, MEHTA v2 beats PEARL-DR + fine-tune (−21.9 ± 1.2 vs −24.8 ± 1.2) but shows no clear difference from compute-matched PEARL + fine-tune (−21.9 ± 1.2 vs −23.5 ± 2.6). The hypothesis required beating both.
  • Its edge is a better starting point, not faster adaptation. MEHTA v2 starts higher on the mirror shift (K=0: −30.8 vs −34.6 and −36.7), but over 400 steps it closes 33% of the gap to the oracle, against 36% for both baselines. Faster post-training is what the MAML step is supposed to provide, and it doesn't show up.
  • It's unstable across seed sets. With actions rotated 60°, MEHTA v2 is now worse than both fine-tuned baselines (−8.4 ± 0.5 vs −6.8 ± 0.6 and −6.5 ± 0.5). On seeds 3–5 it showed no clear difference.
  • The shift really was hard. Every method is still about 18–21 return units short of the oracle (≈ −3.9) after 400 steps. So there was room to win, and no initialization we tried used it.

Conclusion after three rounds (v1; diagnosed v2; v3 on a deliberately hard shift, each on fresh seeds): latent inference plus gradient post-training is the useful combination. Training on perturbed dynamics sometimes helps. A meta-learned initialization for post-training (MEHTA's MAML step) has not shown a reliable gain over fine-tuning from PEARL's own initialization in any of these tests. Seeds 0–8 are now all used.

Ablation: how long should the inner loop be?

MEHTA v2's inner loop runs the test-time post-training protocol, so its length K_inner is also the post-training horizon the initialization is meta-trained for. This sweep changes only K_inner, over 1× to 16× the short-horizon setting (5, 10, 20, 40, 80 steps; the default is 50), on fresh seeds 12–14, run once. Every other hyperparameter is fixed, so longer horizons also cost more compute: each extra inner step adds 2 tasks × 128 samples per iteration. The metric is the return after K=400 post-training steps, mean ± std over seeds. The hypothesis (pre-registered in the spec) was that return rises with K_inner.

Results on fresh seeds 12–14

  • The pre-registered hypothesis is not supported: final return is flat in K_inner. On Point-Goal in-distribution goals, 16× scores −4.57 ± 0.47 against −4.99 ± 0.81 at 1×, which is no clear difference. New goals (−17.1 to −18.0) and shifted dynamics (−6.7 to −7.9) are flat too.
  • Waypoints is noisy with no trend. Waypoints reached ranges from 0.4 to 1.0, with std up to ±0.49; 16× gets 0.60 ± 0.49 against 0.73 ± 0.34 at 1×. The one pair that passes the "beats" rule (2× vs 4×: 1.00 ± 0.28 vs 0.40 ± 0.28) is not part of any trend, and among 10 pairwise comparisons on 3 seeds, one such pass is expected by chance.
  • Longer costs more and buys nothing measurable. 16× takes 2.8× the gradient steps and 1.5× the sample-gradients in meta-training (11.9 M → 17.7 M).

Reading. This agrees with v1 (5 vs 50 steps: no clear difference) and with v2–v3: the MAML step adds little over PEARL's own initialization at this scale, so how far it looks ahead changes little. A horizon effect may still appear on harder tasks, where post-training has more to do (level 2).

What a longer horizon costs

Sample-gradients are exact. Wall-clock and peak memory were measured per run while 10 runs shared one 16-thread CPU with other heavy work (load average around 100), and they are dominated by that load, not by the horizon. Wall-clock varies by up to ±7 minutes between seeds. Peak memory even falls at long horizons (Waypoints 509 → 445 MB), because those runs started first, under different memory pressure. The inner loop clones only the small actor and critics, so the horizon itself adds no memory to speak of. Treat these two as measured, not as benchmarks. Reproduce with uv run python -m mehta.horizon_sweep.

Sim-to-real readiness: physics randomization and shift curves

A policy that will leave the simulator has to survive physics it never saw. This harness trains with domain randomization and then measures how adaptation degrades as the test physics moves away from the training range. It is a wrapper (mehta/randomize.py), so the same code drives Point-Goal, Point-Waypoints and any MuJoCo task that exposes its model.

  • Training: every training episode draws new physics from the ranges above (log-uniform for mass, friction and gain). The physics stays fixed within one task's context, so the latent has something consistent to infer. MEHTA-DR's inner loop adapts on these draws too.
  • Evaluation: held-out goals at shift magnitude s. At s = 1 every parameter sits at the edge of its training range, and s > 1 is outside it (s = 3: mass ×0.51 or ×1.95, noise 0.06, 3 steps of delay). Each task has a fixed random direction of shift, so only the size changes along the curve.
  • Same seed, same physics: a test checks that the wrapper builds identical parameters and noise for the same seed, and that nominal physics reproduces the unwrapped environment exactly.

Shift curves on fresh seeds 9–11

What this does and doesn't show. These are 2-D point masses with a simple viscous-friction model, not a robot. A flat curve here is a necessary check, not evidence of sim-to-real transfer. Still missing for a real robot: a MuJoCo or GPU-simulated task (sim-to-sim across two simulators), contact-rich dynamics, safety limits on the post-trained policy, and real rollouts.

Roadmap: from point masses to robots

  1. Level 2, standard suites. HalfCheetah-Vel/Dir and Meta-World ML1/ML10 with published PEARL/MAML baselines. More seeds (≥5), compute-matched baselines, and a sweep over K_inner ∈ {5, 20, 50, 200}.
  2. Level 3, high fidelity. GPU-parallel simulation with domain randomization over masses, friction and latency. Train in one randomized simulator, test on another (sim-to-sim) as the proxy for sim-to-real.
  3. Level 4, sim to real. Infer z on the robot from a few seconds of data, then run a short, proximally regularized post-training on real rollouts, with safety limits on the post-trained policy.

Everything in this section is a plan. No results above come from these levels.
